## Definition

Hindsight bias is the tendency, once an outcome is known, to believe that it was predictable all along — and to misremember having foreseen it. It is the famous "I knew it all along."

## Mechanism

Once we know how a story ends, our brain integrates that outcome into its understanding of the events that led to it. We build a coherent explanation in which everything fits, and the uncertainty we felt at the time fades from memory. The result then looks inevitable and obvious. We also tend to judge our former selves, and other people, on a clarity that nobody actually had when the decision was made.

## Examples

- After a match, we say "I knew they'd lose" even though we were unsure beforehand.
- After a market crash, commentators explain that "the signs were obvious," although hardly anyone acted on them in time.
- In a post-mortem after a failed project or a medical error, we wonder "how could they not see it?", forgetting that the outcome was unknown to those involved.
- After an exam or a job interview, we are convinced we "had a bad feeling about it" from the start.

## Did you know?

In 1972, ahead of US President Richard Nixon's visits to Beijing and Moscow, psychologists Baruch Fischhoff and Ruth Beyth asked participants to estimate the probability of various possible outcomes of the trips. After the trips, they asked the same people to recall their earlier estimates. Participants remembered having given higher probabilities to events that had actually happened — and lower ones to those that had not. The results were published in [*"I knew it would happen": Remembered probabilities of once-future things*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0030-5073(75)90002-1) (Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 1975). The same year, Fischhoff showed that people who read an account of the 19th-century Anglo-Nepalese War and were told which side won judged that outcome more predictable than those who were not told, a phenomenon he called "creeping determinism" in [*Hindsight ≠ foresight*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.1.3.288) (Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 1975).
