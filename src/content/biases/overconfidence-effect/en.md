## Definition

The overconfidence effect is the tendency for people's confidence in their own judgments, predictions, or knowledge to exceed how accurate those judgments actually turn out to be. It is one of the most robust findings in the psychology of judgment: it affects experts and novices alike, and shows up on both easy and hard questions — though it is strongest on the hardest ones.

## Mechanism

When people gauge their own confidence, they rely on internal cues: how easily an answer comes to mind, how coherent the available information feels, or a general sense of familiarity with the topic. These cues are poor predictors of actual accuracy, partly because people tend to search for reasons that confirm the answer they already lean toward (see confirmation bias) rather than reasons to doubt it. Researchers Don Moore and Paul Healy distinguish three forms of the effect: overestimation of one's own absolute performance, overplacement (believing oneself better than others), and overprecision (excessive certainty that one's estimates are correct) — the last being the most robust and the most studied.

## Examples

- An individual investor is convinced they can "beat the market," even though most professional fund managers themselves fail to do so consistently.
- A project manager announces a delivery date with near-total certainty, which later turns out to be wildly optimistic (see the planning fallacy).
- A student reads through their notes once and feels "100% ready" for the exam, mistaking familiarity with the material for actual mastery of it.
- A doctor sticks to an initial diagnosis with great confidence, even though studies show that clinical confidence does not predict diagnostic accuracy.

## Did you know?

In 1981, Swedish psychologist Ola Svenson asked American and Swedish drivers to rank their own skill and safety relative to other drivers. The result: 93% of Americans and 68% of Swedes placed themselves above the median — a statistically impossible outcome if everyone judged themselves accurately. His paper, [*Are we all less risky and more skillful than our fellow drivers?*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0001-6918(81)90005-6) (Acta Psychologica, 1981), became one of the most cited demonstrations of overconfidence, and has since been replicated in several countries, including in the context of driving itself.
