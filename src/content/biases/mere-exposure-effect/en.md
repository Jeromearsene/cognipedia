## Definition

The mere exposure effect is a cognitive bias whereby repeated exposure to a stimulus — a person, an object, an image, a sound, a made-up word — is enough to increase our preference for it, regardless of its actual qualities. First described by psychologist Robert Zajonc in 1968, it is one of the most robust and widely replicated findings in social psychology: it has been observed with hundreds of different stimuli, across dozens of countries.

## Mechanism

Each new encounter with a stimulus slightly eases its perceptual processing the next time around: this is known as processing fluency. That ease of processing produces a subtle feeling of familiarity, which the brain spontaneously interprets as a positive signal — as if the ease of recognizing something were proof that it's safe, pleasant, or trustworthy. That pleasant feeling then gets attributed to the stimulus itself rather than to its mere repetition. Notably, the effect requires no conscious recognition of the stimulus to occur, and some studies suggest it is even stronger when exposure stays below the threshold of awareness, since explicit recognition can introduce other judgments (boredom, fatigue) that counteract the effect.

## Examples

- A brand runs the same ad dozens of times: consumers end up preferring it over equivalent products, without knowing why.
- A little-known candidate gains popularity simply by appearing very frequently in the media, regardless of the ideas they stand for.
- Most people prefer the mirror-reversed version of their own face in a photo — the one they're most exposed to day to day — while their friends prefer the non-reversed version, which is the one they usually see.
- A coworker passed in the hallway every day, with whom no conversation has ever taken place, ends up seeming friendlier than a stranger encountered only once.

## Did you know?

In 1992, psychologists Richard Moreland and Scott Beach had four women, unknown to the other students, attend the same university lecture course: respectively 0, 5, 10, and 15 times over the semester, without ever interacting with anyone. By the end of the semester, students in the class rated these women as more likable and attractive the more sessions they had attended — even though no exchange had ever taken place. Their study, [*Exposure effects in the classroom: The development of affinity among students*](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031%2892%2990055-O) (Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1992), remains one of the most cited demonstrations of the mere exposure effect outside the laboratory.
