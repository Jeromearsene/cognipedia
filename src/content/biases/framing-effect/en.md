## Definition

The framing effect is the tendency to make different decisions depending on how the same information is presented, for example in terms of gains or losses, even though the objective content is identical.

## Mechanism

We don't compare options in absolute terms: we evaluate them against a reference point that the frame suggests. According to prospect theory, an outcome described as a gain makes us cautious, because we prefer to secure what we have, while an outcome described as a loss makes us willing to take risks to avoid it. The words, percentages or starting point chosen by whoever presents the information therefore steer our judgment, often without us noticing.

## Examples

- A yogurt that is "90% fat-free" seems healthier than one that "contains 10% fat".
- A patient is more willing to accept a surgery described as having a "90% chance of success" than one with a "10% risk of death".
- A shop advertises a "discount" for paying cash rather than a "surcharge" for paying by card: the same price difference is better accepted.
- A headline announces "unemployment falls to 7%" or "7% of the workforce still jobless", and the same figure sounds encouraging or worrying.

## Did you know?

In 1981, psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published [*The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683) in Science. They asked students at Stanford University and the University of British Columbia to imagine that their country was preparing for the outbreak of an unusual Asian disease expected to kill 600 people. In the first group (152 people), Program A would save 200 people for sure, while Program B offered a one-third probability of saving all 600 and a two-thirds probability of saving no one: 72% chose A. In the second group (155 people), Program C meant 400 people would die for sure, while Program D offered a one-third probability that nobody would die and a two-thirds probability that all 600 would die: 78% chose D. Yet A and C are strictly identical, as are B and D: only the wording changed.
