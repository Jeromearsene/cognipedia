## Definition

The framing effect is the tendency to react differently to the same information depending on how it is presented, or "framed" — for instance in terms of gains versus losses — even when the options are logically equivalent.

## Mechanism

Our brain doesn't process information in a purely logical way: it reacts to the packaging as much as to the content. A gain-framed message makes us cautious and risk-averse, while a loss-framed message pushes us to take risks to avoid losing something. This mechanism, described by Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, shows that our choices depend as much on presentation as on actual probabilities.

## Examples

- A medication advertised as "90% success rate" feels more reassuring than the same treatment advertised as "10% failure rate".
- A discount presented as "-20%" is more persuasive than an identical price presented as "pay 80% of the original price".
- A politician announcing a plan will "save 2,000 jobs" is viewed more favorably than one announcing it will "cut 500 out of 2,500 jobs", even though both figures describe the same outcome.

## Did you know?

In 1981, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published a now-famous experiment in the journal *Science*. They presented participants with a fictional scenario: a disease outbreak threatening to kill 600 people, and two programs to respond to it. When the choice was framed in terms of lives saved ("program A saves 200 people"), 72% of participants chose the safe option over the risky one. But when the very same choice was framed in terms of lives lost ("program A, 400 people die"), only 22% still chose the option equivalent to the one before — the majority switched to the risky option. Both versions described the exact same underlying probabilities. This study, published in [*The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683) (Science, 1981), laid the foundations of prospect theory, which would earn Kahneman the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002.
