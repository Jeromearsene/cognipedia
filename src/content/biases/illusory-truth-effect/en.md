## Definition

The illusory truth effect is the tendency to believe that a statement is true merely because it has been encountered or repeated multiple times, independent of its actual accuracy.

## Mechanism

Repetition makes a statement easier to process mentally — a phenomenon called "processing fluency." Because fluent processing feels effortless and familiar, our brain mistakes that ease for a signal of truth: "if it comes to mind so easily, it must be right." This shortcut works even when we hold knowledge that directly contradicts the repeated statement, and even when the statement is clearly implausible or extreme, which shows how hard the effect is to override through deliberate reasoning alone.

## Examples

- A political slogan repeated often across media starts to feel self-evidently true, regardless of its actual content.
- An urban legend or health myth, like "you lose most of your body heat through your head," gets reinforced every time it's casually repeated.
- Advertising slogans repeated across many channels become associated with credibility, not just simple brand recognition.
- On social media, a false claim shared and reshared many times can end up feeling more credible than a single accurate correction.

## Did you know?

In 1977, psychologists Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein, and Thomas Toppino had participants rate the plausibility of 60 trivia-like statements, some true and some false, across three sessions held two weeks apart. Statements repeated across sessions were rated as more believable each time, purely because of the repetition — even though their truth value never changed. This was the first controlled demonstration of what later became known as the illusory truth effect. Decades later, studies on political misinformation found that the effect still works even for statements that blatantly contradict a person's own prior knowledge, which is part of why repeated falsehoods are so hard to correct once they have spread widely.
