## Definition

The Barnum effect, also called the Forer effect, is the tendency to rate vague, general personality descriptions as highly accurate reflections of oneself, even when the same description could apply to almost anyone.

## Mechanism

A statement like "you sometimes doubt whether you made the right decision" is true of nearly everyone, some of the time. Because it is vague, hedged and mostly flattering, each reader unconsciously supplies their own memories and traits to make it fit — then credits the description, rather than their own interpretation, with the accuracy. Trust in the source (an expert, a serious-looking test, a long tradition like astrology) strengthens the effect, and so does a largely positive tone: people are more willing to accept a statement as true of themselves when it is kind.

## Examples

- A horoscope read by millions of people born on different days, each feeling it was written for them.
- A workplace personality test ("you're a natural leader who also values harmony") that employees find strikingly accurate.
- A psychic's "cold reading," where broad guesses are refined based on the client's own reactions.
- A fortune cookie or app-based "daily insight" that feels oddly specific to the person reading it.

## Did you know?

In 1949, psychologist Bertram Forer gave 39 of his students a personality test he called the "Diagnostic Interest Blank," promising each of them a personalized write-up of their results. A week later, every student received the exact same text, lifted almost word for word from a newsstand astrology column — among other lines, "you have a great deal of unused capacity which you have not turned to your advantage." Asked to rate, from 0 to 5, how well it described them personally, students gave it an average of 4.30. The experiment is described in Forer's paper [*The Fallacy of Personal Validation*](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240) (Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1949). The effect later took the name "Barnum effect," coined by psychologist Paul Meehl in reference to showman P. T. Barnum, famous for entertainment "for every taste."
