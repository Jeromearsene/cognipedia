## Definition

The misinformation effect is the tendency for our memory to be altered by misleading information received after an event. The original memory and the suggested information blend together, and we can no longer tell what we actually saw from what we were told.

## Mechanism

Memory is not a faithful recording: it reconstructs the past each time we recall it. When post-event information (a leading question, a news article, another witness's account) seems plausible, it is integrated into the existing memory and partly replaces it. Because we forget where the information came from, we later experience it as an authentic memory, sometimes with very high confidence.

## Examples

- A witness asked leading questions ("Did you see the weapon?") starts remembering a detail they never saw.
- After reading several articles about a news story, people mix up what they saw themselves with what the media reported.
- In a family, the repeated retelling of a childhood memory ends up replacing the real memory of the person concerned.

## Did you know?

In 1974, psychologist Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer, at the University of Washington in Seattle, showed students short films of car accidents. Depending on the verb used in the question ("smashed", "hit" or "contacted"), speed estimates varied by almost 10 mph: about 41 mph with *smashed* versus 32 mph with *contacted*. In a second experiment with 150 students, a week later, 32% of those asked with the verb *smashed* said they had seen broken glass, although there was none in the film (versus 14% with *hit* and 12% in the control group): a single changed verb was enough to create a false memory. Loftus devoted her career to the phenomenon, and her work has been widely discussed in the context of eyewitness testimony in court: [*Reconstruction of automobile destruction*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3) (Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 1974).
