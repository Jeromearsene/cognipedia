## Definition

The misinformation effect is the tendency of our memory to be altered by misleading or suggestive information received after an event. The memory becomes a blend of what we actually experienced and what we were told afterwards, and we can't tell one from the other.

## Mechanism

Our memory isn't a video recording: every time we recall something, we rebuild it. Information picked up in the meantime (an investigator's question, a news article, a relative's account) blends into the original memory. We also forget where each detail came from, and mix up what we saw with what we were told. The altered memory feels just as vivid and clear, and we defend it with the same confidence.

## Examples

- A witness asked "did you see the attacker's knife?" may start to remember a knife that was never there.
- After talking over a shared memory (a wedding, a holiday) with relatives, we unknowingly absorb the details others told, and "see" them as if we had lived them.
- After a heavily covered event, we remember images or details from the press as if we had observed them ourselves.
- During an interrogation or interview, leading or repeated questions can end up producing details the person never retained in the first place.

## Did you know?

In 1974, psychologist Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed 45 students films of car accidents, then asked them how fast the vehicles were going when they "smashed" or "contacted" each other. With "smashed", participants estimated 40.8 miles per hour on average, against 31.8 with "contacted". In a second experiment, 150 students were questioned the same way, then a week later asked whether they had seen any broken glass. There was none in the film, yet 32% of those who had read "smashed" said yes, against 14% with "hit" and 12% for the group that had not been asked about speed. The results were published in [*Reconstruction of automobile destruction*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3) (Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 1974). Twenty years later, Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell went further: with 24 participants, they had a relative tell four childhood memories, one of them invented from scratch (getting lost in a shopping mall). About a quarter of them ended up "remembering" it, some adding details of their own. The study is described in [*The formation of false memories*](https://doi.org/10.3928/0048-5713-19951201-07) (Psychiatric Annals, 1995).
