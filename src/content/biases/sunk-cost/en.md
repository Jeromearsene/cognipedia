## Definition

The sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to keep pursuing an action or investment solely because time, money, or energy has already been invested in it, even when continuing is no longer reasonable.

## Mechanism

A sunk cost is already lost: rationally, it should not influence what comes next. But giving up means admitting a loss, which is psychologically painful (see loss aversion). So we persevere to avoid having "lost everything for nothing," even if it means losing even more. The desire to stay consistent with our past decisions and the fear of looking like someone who made a mistake add to the pull.

## Examples

- We finish a mediocre meal at a restaurant because we paid for it.
- We keep repairing an old car that constantly breaks down because we've already spent so much on it.
- A team pushes on with a doomed software project "because we've already spent six months on it."
- We stay in a relationship or a course that no longer suits us because of the years already invested.

## Did you know?

On November 29, 1962, in London, France and the United Kingdom signed the treaty launching the Concorde supersonic airliner. Over the years, costs ballooned and orders collapsed, notably after the 1973 oil shock. Yet both governments kept funding the project, partly because of the sums already committed. The aircraft entered service in January 1976, and only around twenty were ever built. Biologist Richard Dawkins and Tamsin Carlisle saw it as an example of a reasoning error they dubbed the "Concorde fallacy" in their paper [*Parental investment, mate desertion and a fallacy*](https://doi.org/10.1038/262131b0) (Nature, 1976) — hence the name still used today for the sunk cost fallacy.
